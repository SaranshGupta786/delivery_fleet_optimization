## Objective (what every strategy minimises)

```text
objective = distance cost (km x each vehicle's cost_per_km)
          + 2.00 x lateness minutes
          + 60 x priority weight for every refused request
          + 0.05 x makespan minutes
```

Hard constraints (never traded): capability match, capacity in kg and m3 across the whole route, service inside the window plus grace, shift end, route-length cap, and at most one vehicle per request. Lateness inside the grace band is priced rather than forbidden, which is what lets strategies trade coverage against punctuality.

## Findings

* **rl[+polish] leads on average**: mean objective 2779.3, mean gap to the per-scenario best +20.8%, worst case +81.6%, 4 of 10 scenarios won outright.
* The ranking is regime-dependent rather than absolute - per-preset leaders by mean objective: `small` -> regret[+polish] (best single book: greedy[deadline]); `medium` -> regret[+polish] (best single book: rl[+polish]); `tight` -> rl[+polish] (best single book: greedy[deadline]); `stress` -> rl[+polish] (best single book: greedy[priority]); `large` -> regret[+polish] (best single book: regret[+polish]). Read the per-preset tables below instead of trusting a single global ordering; constrained and comfortable books reward different behaviour.
* The mechanism is visible in `make capacity` (CAPACITY_SWEEP.md): hold the book size fixed and tighten only the fleet's capacity, and the learned policy overtakes the hand-tuned regret rule at 1.0x capacity, then widens its lead monotonically - regret's fixed distance/lateness trade-off collapses under refusal pressure while the learned policy degrades gracefully. That is the evidence behind the engine's problem statement: fleet capacity is the scarce resource, and the policy that learns the coverage-vs-punctuality-vs-refusal trade-off is the one that wins.
* The relocate local search is worth 824.9 objective points on average (4465.4 plain -> 3640.5 polished), which is the single biggest lever after the request-selection rule itself.
* Hungarian batch matching minimises distance, not the objective: adding a lateness term to the batch cost moves its mean gap from +141.5% to +87.9%, which is why the objective - not distance alone - drives every decision here.
* Learning does real work: the trained policy scores 3110.3 against 6234.6 for an untrained argmax policy (50.1% better), so the weights - not the action ordering - carry the result.
* Learned policy + shared polish scores 2779.3 versus 4465.4 for the hand-tuned heuristic without polish: the learned selection rule is competitive with the hand-written one when both get the same post-processing.
* Against the exhaustive optimum on 5-request instances, the worst heuristic gap is +127.9% and no heuristic ever beats the optimum, which is the sanity check for the scoring itself.
* Committed policy provenance: 2500 episodes, seed 7, 1386 s of training, holdout objective 711.8 -> 615.8.

## What this does not show

* No real traffic, road network or live order feed: travel is straight-line haversine at a constant cruising speed, so urban distances are optimistic.
* The learned policy is a 17-feature linear softmax, not a sequence model: it scores insertions, it does not learn a distribution over permutations.
* Scenario difficulty is synthetic. The ranking of strategies is the informative part; the absolute numbers are not currency.
* Timings are single-process wall clock on the machine that ran the benchmark, so they compare strategies to each other rather than against any service-level target.
